The problem

A Minecraft network isn't one server. A proxy sits at the front and hands players off to whichever backend they've chosen, which means no single server ever sees a player's whole session. Each one only knows about the slice of time that player spent on it. If you want to answer something as basic as "where has this person actually been tonight?", there's nowhere to ask.

What I built

A plugin that lives on the Velocity proxy rather than on any one backend, so it sits at the only point in the network that sees every transfer. It follows players across the servers behind the proxy, records what they do, and surfaces that while they're still online instead of leaving it in a log to read later.

  • Tracks players across every server behind the proxy, not per-server
  • Logs activity as it happens, keyed to the player rather than the box
  • Reports back in real time, so it's useful during a session and not just after one

Why this one

It was my Computer Science coursework, but I picked it because it was a problem I already had on my own network — not an exercise invented to be marked. That made the requirements easy to write and the testing honest: either it told me something true about my players or it didn't.

What it taught me

This was the project where Java stopped being a syllabus language. Working against the Velocity API meant reading real documentation, dealing with events firing in an order I didn't choose, and thinking about what happens to state when a player disconnects mid-transfer. It also made me a lot more careful about what's worth recording in the first place.

What it is

My own Minecraft network — the thing every other item on this site connects back to. There's no team to hand parts of it to, so I do all of it: the server logic, the hosting, and the content that gives anyone a reason to join in the first place.

Building it

The gameplay systems are written in Skript, which is where most of my server-side logic lives. A Velocity proxy sits in front as the front door, and everything runs on infrastructure I look after myself rather than a panel someone else controls.

  • Custom server systems written and maintained in Skript
  • Hosting, updates and uptime — including the parts that break at inconvenient times
  • Content and media made around the network, not bolted on afterwards

Running it

Owning a network is mostly the unglamorous half: working out why something died, deciding whether a feature is worth the maintenance it will cost, and keeping players informed when it's down. Doing development and community at the same time means I feel the consequences of my own technical decisions quite quickly.

What it taught me

Almost everything I know about systems came from here. Self-hosting Brickworks is what pushed me into Linux, Docker and networking properly, and it's the reason I write code with "what happens when this fails at 2am" in mind.

The role

I'm Media Manager and one of the General Managers at Zyralis. The media half is content direction — deciding what gets made, how the network presents itself, and keeping that consistent. The GM half is the day-to-day of actually running a network with other people in it.

What I do

  • Set and hold the content direction across the network's media
  • Work with the community directly, rather than through announcements only
  • Handle day-to-day operations and the decisions that can't wait for a meeting

Why it's different to Brickworks

On Brickworks I answer to myself. At Zyralis I'm working inside someone else's network with a team, which means writing things down, agreeing on direction before acting, and handing work over cleanly. It's been the more useful of the two for learning how to work with people instead of just around them.

What it taught me

That the community side isn't separate from the technical side. A feature nobody understands might as well not exist, and an outage handled well costs you less than a quiet one handled badly.
